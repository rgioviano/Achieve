## 1. Wang et al. (2026) — VBT vs %1RM (PBT)

**Cite:** Wang Y, Qiu J, Dai D, Li Q, Wang X, Luo Q. The effects of velocity-based vs. percentage-based resistance training on sports performance in trained individuals: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil*. 2026. DOI: [10.1186/s13102-025-01504-9](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-025-01504-9)  
**Design / sample / limits:** Meta of 17 controlled trials, n=348 trained (≥6 mo RT, age ≥16). Interventions ≥4 wk. Most VL cutoffs 10–30%. Most interventions ≤8 wk; age mostly 16–30. GRADE moderate.

**Findings (prescription):**
- VBT beat PBT for **jump** (SMD 0.27) and **change of direction** (SMD 0.45).
- **Max strength** and **sprint** similar between VBT and PBT.
- Typical VL band in included studies: **10–30%**.

**If → then**
- If goal is jump or COD → prefer velocity feedback / VL stop over fixed %1RM alone.
- If goal is max strength only → either method is fine when volume/intensity matched.
- Keep power sets near **~10% VL** (aligns with Achieve power rule + last week’s Wang Front Physiol adolescent sprinter finding).

## 2. Feng, Wang & Wang (2026) — Concurrent sequence

**Cite:** Feng Z, Wang Y, Wang J. The effects, mechanisms, and influencing factors of concurrent strength and endurance training with different sequences: a semi-systematic review. *Front Sports Act Living*. 2026. DOI: [10.3389/fspor.2025.1692399](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2025.1692399)  
**Design / sample / limits:** Semi-systematic, 42 studies (26 human trials + reviews + animal). Search to Dec 2024. Human samples often small / 8–12 wk. Animal molecular data do not map cleanly to humans.

**Findings (prescription):**
- Sequence does **not** clearly change hypertrophy, max strength, or VO₂max in humans.
- **Strength → endurance** better for relative strength / explosive power / neuromuscular adaptation.
- If endurance must come first → leave **≥3 h** between modalities.
- Adults/older: prefer strength-first. Adolescents: some evidence favors aerobic-then-strength (weaker; use cautiously).

**If → then**
- Same-day dryland + swim/run: lift (or power) first when explosiveness matters.
- Must do cardio first → wait ≥3 h before heavy/power lower body.
- Don’t rearrange order hoping for bigger 1RM or muscle size — order mainly protects power quality.

## 3. Tundidor-Duque et al. (2026) — Concurrent in team sports

**Cite:** Tundidor-Duque R, Pareja-Blanco F, Drozd M, Pereira LA, Loturco I. Factors influencing the efficacy of concurrent training in team sports: a narrative review. *J Hum Kinet*. Published 2026-02-05. PubMed: [41766799](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41766799/)  
**Design / sample / limits:** Narrative review of 13 experimental team-sport studies (youth + senior, M/F). Not a meta. Congested-schedule context.

**Findings (prescription):**
- Same session: **strength before endurance**.
- Separate by **≥6 h** → strength/speed gains preserved similar to longer gaps; **~24 h** may help VO₂max more.
- High endurance volume / no rest between modalities blunts speed/power more than max strength.
- Training status alone is not decisive if recovery and load are controlled; interference hits power hardest in higher-level athletes.

**If → then**
- Dual-session athlete days: strength/power AM, conditioning PM (≥6 h) when possible.
- Same session with short rest: never put hard cardio before quality power/strength work.
- Protect jump/sprint quality first; 1RM can hold while power drops if sequencing is wrong.

## 4. Zhao et al. (2025) — French contrast training (FCT)

**Cite:** Zhao Z, Ma Z, Wu C, Zheng X, Liu T, Deng N, Zhou K. The effects of French contrast training on lower limb athletic performance in healthy adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Front Physiol*. 2025. DOI: [10.3389/fphys.2025.1672353](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2025.1672353)  
**Design / sample / limits:** 7 RCTs, n=195 (mostly ~19–22 y athletes). 8–12 wk. Moderate GRADE. Small trials; not clearly superior to traditional complex training; no significant max-strength advantage vs matched controls.

**Protocol pattern (4-step set):**
1. Heavy compound ~**80–85% 1RM** (e.g. squat 2–5 reps)
2. Bodyweight plyo (depth/box jump)
3. Light–moderate velocity load ~**25–30% 1RM** (jump squat)
4. Assisted / accelerated plyo (band-assisted jump)

**Dose:** **2–3×/wk**, **8–12 wk**, often **3–4** contrast sets.

**Findings:** Large sprint benefit (SMD −0.92), moderate jump benefit (SMD 0.62) vs routine/equal-load traditional RT. **2×/wk** favored jumps; **3×/wk** favored sprint in subgroups. Not a max-strength method.

**If → then**
- Need jump/sprint block without replacing heavy strength days → add FCT 2×/wk for 8+ weeks (jumps) or 3×/wk if sprint is the priority.
- Keep separate heavy strength days if 1RM is still a goal — FCT does not replace them.
- Prefer fixed high loads in the heavy slot over soft progressive loading when jump is the target.
- Do not claim FCT beats standard heavy→plyo complex yet — evidence insufficient.

## 5. Zhu et al. (2026) — Flywheel dosing for jump/sprint

**Cite:** Zhu Z, Yue T, Sun R, He J, Lin W. Optimizing the dose of flywheel resistance training for jumping and sprinting performance: a systematic review and meta-analysis of frequency, volume, and duration. *Sci Rep*. 2026. DOI: [10.1038/s41598-026-62815-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-62815-6)  
**Design / sample / limits:** 12 studies, n=280 athletes. FRT vs conventional. GRADE per outcomes not fully detailed in abstract page; dose subgroups from meta-regression style cuts.

**Findings (prescription):**
- Small edge vs conventional for jump (g=0.35) and sprint (g=−0.32).
- Meaningful dose: **≥8–9 weeks**, **≥10 total sessions**, cumulative volume (sets×reps×sessions) **≥210** (jump) / **≥244** (sprint).
- **1–2 sessions/week** beat higher weekly frequency (recovery for eccentric load).

**If → then**
- Programming flywheel for jump/sprint → plan **≥8 weeks**, **1–2×/wk**, accumulate enough total reps (≥210 jump / ≥244 sprint), not more frequent short blocks.
- In-season: prefer low frequency over stacking 3+ flywheel days.

## 6. Winwood et al. (2026) — Taper / peaking in weight-lifting sports

**Cite:** Winwood PW, Travis SK, Barnes MJ, Keogh JWL, Pritchard HJ. Tapering and peaking in the weight lifting sports: a systematic review of athletes’ self-reported strategies. *Sports Med*. 2026. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-026-02500-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02500-w)  
**Design / sample / limits:** Systematic review of 7 high-quality survey/interview studies (CrossFit, Highland Games, powerlifting, strongman, weightlifting). Describes practice, not RCTs of taper efficacy. Adult competitors.

**Findings (prescription):**
- Typical: **step taper**, **5–9 days**, volume down **~40–45%**, intensity peak **2–3 weeks** out, volume peak **4–6 weeks** out.
- Training stop before meet: **~1.5–4 days**.
- Last heavy (>85%): deadlift **6–8 d**, squat **~5–6.5 d**, bench **3–5 d** before competition (practice pattern; lab data do not prove deadlift needs more recovery).
- Most common taper mistake: bad load management + life stress (work/travel/nutrition), not “wrong” taper type.

**If → then**
- Strength/power peaking (IB, adult strength athletes, meet week): use a **short step unload (~1 week)**, cut volume ~40–45%, keep key intensity earlier in the week, last heavy lower ~5–8 days out.
- Do not copy 2–4 week endurance tapers for barbell/power athletes.
- Swim meet weeks: same idea — cut dryland volume hard for ~5–9 days; keep a few sharp intent sets rather than grinding volume.
