## 1. Theodorou et al. (2026): how long one hard eccentric session protects you from soreness and strength loss. [FULL TEXT] PROGRAMMING

**Cite:** Theodorou AA, Margaritelis NV, Karyofyllidou AV, Christodoulou F, Jamurtas AZ, Fatouros IG, Kyparos A, Nikolaidis MG, Paschalis V. The Duration of the Repeated-Bout Effect After Eccentric Exercise. *Scand J Med Sci Sports*. 2026;36(10):e70381. https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70381 (open access, PMC13674372)
**Design / sample:** 48 recreationally active young men (about 22–24 y), 12 per group, sample size set in advance, registered (NCT07503860). Everyone did one maximal eccentric quad session: 5 × 15 reps on an isokinetic machine at 60°/s, 2 min rest. Each group repeated the same session after 3, 6, 9 or 12 weeks, with no hard eccentric work in between. Measures on days 2 and 4: isometric strength, soreness, range of motion, muscle swelling (ultrasound), CK, CRP.
**Who it helps at Achieve:** anyone coming back to Nordics, flywheel, heavy lowering work or deceleration-heavy sport after a break: off-season returners, athletes who step down in-season, adults back from a layoff.

**What it found (numbers):**
- **3 weeks:** second session caused much less damage. Strength was 32.7 Nm (day 2) and 24.5 Nm (day 4) higher than after the first session, and soreness was 2.5 and 2.2 points lower on a 1–10 scale.
- **6 weeks:** still protected. Strength 22.0 / 21.6 Nm higher; soreness 1.2 / 1.7 points lower.
- **9 weeks:** partial. Strength still 22.4 Nm higher on day 2 (day 4 not significant). Soreness was no longer different.
- **12 weeks:** no protection on any measure. Strength difference −9.4 / +2.8 Nm (ns). Equivalence tests (exploratory, margins picked after the fact) supported "no meaningful protection" for strength, soreness, range of motion, swelling and ultrasound. CK and CRP were inconclusive.
- For scale (my arithmetic): baseline strength was about 211–229 Nm, so the protection was worth roughly 10–15% of baseline strength in the days after.

**Prescription first:**
- If an athlete hasn't done hard eccentric work for **~9–12+ weeks**, treat the next session as a **first exposure**. Use a low intro dose, and keep it **at least 4 days before a game, meet or test day** (damage was still present at day 4).
- To keep the protection, give a **hard eccentric touch at least every ~6 weeks** (inference: the authors suggest periodic doses, but the study only tested single sessions).
- One number: **protection is solid at 6 weeks, patchy at 9, gone at 12.**
- **Don't change:** current eccentric progressions inside a continuous block. This paper is about gaps.

**How it could fit Achieve:** plan a "re-entry" eccentric week after the winter break and after in-season step-downs. Put the first hard Nordic or flywheel session early in the week, not 1–2 days before competition.

**Limits / unknowns:** young recreational men only. One machine-based quad protocol, harder than typical gym work. Only days 2 and 4 were measured. Elbow-flexor studies the authors cite found protection lasting much longer (Nosaka: some markers up to 9 months), so the timeline may differ by muscle. Hamstrings were not tested (inference that it applies to Nordics). No youth or female data.

## 2. Franke et al. (2026): one set of 6 Nordics a week did NOT hold preseason hamstring gains in-season. [ABSTRACT ONLY] PROGRAMMING

**Cite:** Franke RA, Tremea CEM, Rebolledo-Cobos RC, Nakamura FY, Baroni BM. Can in-season Nordic hamstring exercise microdosing retain eccentric hamstring strength? *Int J Sports Med*. Published online 2026-10-16. https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2982-7578 (PMID 42887562)
**Design / sample:** 34 athletes did a 5-week preseason Nordic program (up to 60 reps/week). They were then randomized to no Nordics in-season, or a microdose of **1 set × 6 Nordics per week**, for 10 in-season weeks. Eccentric hamstring strength was tested at baseline, end of preseason, and in-season weeks 5 and 10.
**Who it helps at Achieve:** sprint and field athletes in-season, especially anyone on the optional 1x/week in-season step-down.

**What it found (numbers):**
- Preseason gain: **+9.5%** (95% CI 5.3–14.3%). Microdose group 317 → 346 N; control 292 → 321 N.
- By **in-season week 5, both groups were back to baseline** (microdose 324 N; control 289 N), and they stayed there at week 10 (330 N; 290 N).
- **No difference between groups.** 6 reps a week was not enough to keep the gain.

**Prescription first:**
- Don't count on **1 × 6 Nordics/week** to hold hamstring strength in-season.
- If hamstring strength matters in-season, give it a bigger weekly dose than that. This study doesn't say what dose works (inference: more sets, or 2 exposures/week where the schedule allows).
- For 1x/week step-down athletes, make that one session count: put real hamstring volume in it, not a token set (inference).
- One number: **gains gone by week 5** of the season.
- **Don't change:** the preseason Nordic build. That part worked (+9.5% in 5 weeks).

**How it could fit Achieve:** retest eccentric hamstring strength about 5 weeks into a season to catch the drop. Note that Hawkin plates don't measure Nordic force directly; the device used here isn't named in the abstract.

**Limits / unknowns:** abstract only. Sport, sex, age and in-season sport load are not given. Small trial (34 split two ways). Only one microdose level was tested.
**Fits / disagrees:** Sundh 2026 (already briefed) found hamstring and sprint measures mostly held over a **2-week** break after a low-volume Nordic + isometric block. Franke's loss showed up by **5 weeks**, so the two don't directly conflict; they cover different time spans. Together with Theodorou (#1), the inference is that a small weekly dose might keep athletes from getting sore when reloaded, but it did not keep the strength. That pairing is untested.

## 3. Martos-Arregui et al. (2026): training the weaker leg first, or giving it more volume. [FULL TEXT] PROGRAMMING + TESTING/PLATE

**Cite:** Martos-Arregui A, Rojas-Cepero I, Zhang X, García-Ramos A, Rojas-Ruiz FJ, Cepero-González M. Are Asymmetry-Focused Training Interventions More Effective than General Programs in Reducing Interlimb Asymmetries and Improving Athletic Performance? A Systematic Review. *Sports Med Open*. 2026;12(1):156. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-026-01119-5 (open access, PMC13661881)
**Design / sample:** systematic review (no meta-analysis) of 11 studies, 297 athletes aged 12–30, mostly male (soccer, basketball, karate, handball). Study sizes ranged from 18 to 66. Interventions lasted ≥4 weeks. PROSPERO-registered. Risk of bias was often unclear: only 4/11 had clearly low-risk randomization and only 3 blinded testers; 4 had 20–40%+ dropout.
**Who it helps at Achieve:** youth and HS athletes with a real left–right gap on single-leg jumps or strength, and return-from-injury athletes cleared to train.

**What it found:**
- **Starting each single-leg set with the weaker leg** gave "modest but consistent" benefits for closing the gap and for performance in most studies. One study (Moreno-Azze, U-17 soccer) found no between-group difference.
- **Double volume on the weaker leg** (3 studies, two of them on the same 35 players) often beat simply starting with it.
- **Triple volume** on the weaker leg: 2 studies, positive but weak (control training poorly described in one).
- **Starting with the stronger leg:** no consistent benefit.
- **Single-leg vs two-leg training:** each mostly improved its own kind of test. Single-leg work improved single-leg tests; two-leg work improved two-leg tests. Only 1 of 4 studies showed single-leg training shrinking the gap more.
- Example protocol (Gonzalo-Skok, elite basketball, 16.8 y, 6 weeks, 2x/week): single-leg 90° squat 3 sets with a ~10% power-drop cutoff, single-leg drop jump from 0.25 m 2×5, single-leg CMJ 2×5, 3 min rest, weaker leg first.
- **No included study checked that the starting gap was bigger than the test's own noise.**

**Prescription first:**
- Find the weaker leg **with the same task you're training** (e.g., single-leg jump on the plates for jump work), not by asking which leg they kick with.
- **Start every single-leg set with the weaker leg.** It costs nothing.
- If the gap is real and persistent, **add 1 extra set for the weaker leg** (the double-volume idea; evidence is thin).
- Keep two-leg lifts in. They still drive two-leg performance.
- One number: common cutoffs are **10–15%**, but the authors say there's no universal threshold. Use the test's own noise first.

**TESTING/PLATE (for Hawkin):** before flagging an asymmetry, confirm it is bigger than the test's typical variation for that athlete or test. Retest the same single-leg task to see whether it closed. Asymmetry direction can flip between tests and over time.

**Limits / unknowns:** small, mostly male team-sport samples. No pooled effect sizes. Overlapping samples. Different formulas for asymmetry. No swimmers. The link to injury risk is assumed, not tested here.

## 4. Resisted sprint training: two new reviews agree on short acceleration and disagree on jumps. [ABSTRACT ONLY, both] PROGRAMMING

**Cite A:** Li C, Chen L, Zhang Q, Xin X, Li X. Is Training Transfer Uniform? A Systematic Review and Three-Level Meta-Analysis of Resisted Sprint Training Effects on Acceleration, Jumping, and Change of Direction Performance in Athletes. *Scand J Med Sci Sports*. 2026;36(10):e70375. https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70375 (abstract only; the publisher page showed the abstract and reference list, but the body did not load)
**Cite B:** Bruno J, Montoro-Bombú R, Thapa RK, Sarmento H. Effects of Resisted Sprint Training and Sprint Performance and Related Physical Capacities: An Umbrella Review of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses. *Sports Med*. Published online 2026-10-06. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02538-w (abstract only, closed access)
**Design / sample:** A: 66 studies, 1,945 participants; 31 controlled studies (179 effect sizes) in the main analysis; GRADE rated. B: 15 meta-analyses (6–157 studies each, 109–978 participants); AMSTAR 2 quality (5 high, 10 low); GRADE ranged from very low to high.
**Who it helps at Achieve:** field and court athletes (HS and youth) whose sport is decided in the first 10–20 m.

**What they found (numbers):**
- **A:** resisted sprints helped acceleration (g 0.32, 95% CI 0.16–0.49), vertical jump (g 0.25), horizontal jump (g 0.24) and change of direction (g 0.46). **Compared with plain sprint training, the extra gain was small (g 0.22–0.30).** No load, dose or method (sled, vest, etc.) stood out. Certainty: moderate for acceleration, low for everything else. Mostly male athletes.
- **B:** the most consistent benefit was **short acceleration, ≤20 m**. Change of direction and leg stiffness gains were mixed. **Top speed, jumps and sprint mechanics: inconsistent.** No best load or method. Best evidence comes from young male team-sport athletes.
- **Disagreement:** A finds small jump gains; B calls jump results inconsistent. Both agree there's no magic sled load.

**Prescription first:**
- Use sleds or vests as an **add-on for 0–20 m acceleration**, alongside unresisted sprints, not instead of them.
- **Pick the load by what you want and what the athlete can sprint well with.** Neither review found a best load (inference: coach choice is fine).
- Don't sell resisted sprints as a top-speed or vertical-jump tool.
- One number: **the extra gain over plain sprinting is small (g ~0.2–0.3).**
- **Don't change:** keep max-effort unresisted sprinting as the base.

**Limits / unknowns:** both abstract only, so I couldn't check study-level loads or youth subgroups. The female evidence base is thin. Not relevant to swimmers.

## 5. Puschkasch-Möck, Haff, Behringer & Wirth (2026): a heavy squat block vs a heavy block finishing with optimal-power jump squats. [ABSTRACT ONLY] PROGRAMMING + TESTING/PLATE

**Cite:** Puschkasch-Möck S, Haff GG, Behringer M, Wirth K. Does a dedicated optimal power phase enhance performance outcomes in trained athletes? A comparison of periodized maximum strength and strength–power training. *Eur J Appl Physiol*. Published online 2026-10-14. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-026-06477-2 (abstract only, closed access)
**Design / sample:** 80 trained athletes (62 M, 18 F) from various sports, matched by baseline jump height into 4 groups, over 14 weeks. The groups were: block-periodized back squat (SQ); the same program but with jump squats at each athlete's best-power load in the final block (JS); isokinetic leg press (ISO); and control.
**Who it helps at Achieve:** HS and adult athletes in a strength-to-power progression.

**What it found:**
- Squat 1RM (absolute and relative) rose in **SQ and JS**. Control dropped. **Isokinetic leg press: no change.**
- **Jump height rose only in the heavy-squat group.**
- **The JS group improved drop-jump RSI (20–30 cm boxes) and loaded jump-squat power at 40–80% 1RM.**
- Overall the authors found **no clear winner** between the two full programs.
- No effect sizes in the abstract.

**Prescription first:**
- A final optimal-power jump-squat block **keeps the strength gains and adds quick-contact (RSI) and loaded-power gains**. In this study it did **not** add CMJ height beyond heavy squatting.
- If CMJ height is the target, heavy squatting alone moved it here.
- If quick ground contacts matter (sprint, court), finishing with a jump-squat block is reasonable.
- **Don't use an isokinetic leg press as the main lower-body strength tool** (no gains).
- One number: **RSI moved at 20–30 cm drop heights.**

**TESTING/PLATE (for Hawkin):** test what the block trains. After a power block, check drop-jump RSI from 20–30 cm, not just CMJ height. A flat CMJ doesn't mean the block failed. This matches last week's Holík finding that CMJ height missed changes that other tests caught.

**Limits / unknowns:** abstract only. Block lengths, jump-squat loads, rep schemes and effect sizes are unknown. About 20 per group (inference: 80 ÷ 4). Mixed sports and training ages.
**Fits / disagrees:** Zhao 2025 (already briefed) found French contrast beat traditional training for jumps. Here a separate power block didn't add jump height over heavy squats. These are different methods (contrast sets vs a dedicated block), so it's tension, not a direct contradiction.

## Performance Coach: integrate this week
1. **Eccentric re-entry rule:** if hard eccentric work has been off ≥9–12 weeks, start low and keep it ≥4 days from competition. Keep a hard eccentric touch at least every ~6 weeks.
2. **In-season Nordics:** 1 × 6/week didn't hold gains (gone by week 5). Give more weekly hamstring work in-season, especially in 1x/week step-down sessions.
3. **Weaker leg first** on all single-leg work. Consider +1 set for a real, persistent gap.
4. **Sleds/vests** = small add-on for 0–20 m acceleration. Keep plain sprints as the base. No best load.
5. **Power block:** finishing a strength block with optimal-power jump squats keeps strength and adds RSI and loaded power, but don't expect extra CMJ height. Skip isokinetic leg press as the main strength tool.

## Hawkin: testing notes
- Judge left–right gaps against each test's own noise before acting. Retest the same single-leg task.
- After power or quick-contact blocks, add a drop-jump RSI check (20–30 cm). CMJ height can stay flat.
