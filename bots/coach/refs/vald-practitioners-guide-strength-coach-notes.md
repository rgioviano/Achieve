## Core program-design frameworks (actionable rules)

### Define the decision before the workout
- Program design = strategic organization of training variables to develop physical qualities for performance, rehab, or long-term prep.
- Before exercises or load: define goals/outcomes so success is compared to the original plan, not vibes.
- Answer three questions every block: (1) What physical qualities should be developed? (2) How will they be measured? (3) Is the current program producing the intended adaptations?

### Needs analysis first
- Needs analysis = where the athlete/team is strong vs limited relative to activity demands.
- Reverse-engineer sport → physical determinants → KPIs → training methods.
- Use objective profile + norms/benchmarks (e.g., VALD Norms) to decide where resources go. Do not train everything equally.

### Strength as the common denominator
- Focus determinants typically: strength, power, endurance, speed, agility, mobility.
- Progress in one quality is often bottlenecked by lack of capacity in another; muscular strength is frequently that bottleneck and underpins many injury-prevention strategies.
- Strength is task-specific: use multiple distinct assessments (not one lift) for a complete maximal-force picture (guide examples: Nordic peak force, isometric squat peak force via NordBord / ForceDecks).

### Vermeil’s performance model (referenced)
- Assessment + strength development sit in the foundation of performance; explosiveness, reactive strength, and speed are developed/expressed relative to that foundation.

### Workload equation
- Three basic components: **intensity** (load, speed, intent relative to a max), **volume** (sets/reps/distance/amount), **frequency** (sessions in a period).
- All three create workload; **workload × recovery** determines adaptability (Scott Dye envelope-of-function framing: load and frequency vs adaptable thresholds).
- Volume and frequency are often constrained by intensity when programming athletes.

### Profiling vs monitoring (do not confuse them)

| Type | Purpose | Metric style (guide examples) |
|------|---------|-------------------------------|
| **Profiling** | Maximal capacities; periodic; informs future program design | Peak force, sprint time, jump height (tied to quality of interest) |
| **Monitoring** | Neuromuscular status; frequent; readiness + acute fatigue | RSI-Mod, contraction time, asymmetry |

### Adaptation models that justify monitoring
- **General Adaptation Syndrome:** alarm → adaptation → exhaustion if recovery insufficient.
- **Fitness–Fatigue Model:** performance = fitness adaptations minus temporary fatigue.

### Adaptation principles (apply with data, not slogans)
- **Specificity:** exposures must align with the desired outcome. Specificity can target kinetics (force, braking, RFD, impulse), kinematics (patterns, positions, velocities), tissue capacity, energy systems, and/or task/environment constraints. Overlap is expected; prioritize the adaptations that matter for *this* phase.
- **Progressive overload:** increase workload gradually via intensity, volume, frequency, *or* constraints (ROM, technique, velocity). Guide progression with feedback, not fixed calendars alone.
- **Individuality:** training age, injury history, anthropometrics, skill, physiology, and psychosocial factors change tolerance/adaptation. Identical programs → different outcomes. Individualize from assessment + monitoring + wellness, not from a “perfect” template.
- **Reversibility:** unused qualities decay. Strength often holds longer than muscle mass (neural adaptations persist). Rough retention: strength may show minimal decline in the first **2–4 weeks** of inactivity; mass can drift sooner as MPS returns toward baseline. Use decay rates to set reassessment windows and phase sequences.

### Amplify adaptations by combining methods
- Heavy resistance training raises peak isometric force (e.g., IMTP).
- Plyometrics raise RFD (e.g., DJ/CMJ on force plates).
- Either alone: ~**2–7%** jump-height improvement after **6–12 weeks**.
- Integrating HSR + plyometrics in the same program typically beats either alone for jump performance.

## Exercise selection & session structure

### Default session order (ascending)
- Put explosive/plyometric work **early**, before fatigue accumulates; maximal intent + minimal fatigue → better neuromuscular performance.
- Default sequence: high-velocity explosive work → heavier compound strength → accessories later for volume/hypertrophy targets.
- **Exception — PAPE:** heavy conditioning activity before explosives to potentiate power. Often impractical in routine sessions because of long recovery between conditioning and explosives.

### Simple vs advanced power programs (context from guide)
- **In-season / developing:** simple mix (squats, sprints, jumps, isometrics) can maintain/improve power depending on sport and training history.
- **Off-season / established:** more advanced tools (supersets, contrasting stimuli, higher volumes) may be required.

### Strength methods (select from need, not novelty)
- Heavy resistance training
- Isometrics
- Eccentric overload
- Heavy-slow resistance (HSR)
- Unilateral loading

Nearly all can improve performance if dosed well; dosage is the hard part.

### Power methods
- Olympic variations, loaded jumps, ballistic resistance, med-ball throws, accelerative-intent resistance training.
- Slower/higher-impact plyometrics (loaded CMJs, hurdle jumps, higher DJs) bias force and peak power.
- Combining **slow and fast** SSC work increases power adaptations.
- SSC timing cue from guide: slow SSC **>250 ms** ground contact; fast SSC **<250 ms** (both can improve RFD, neural drive, stiffness).

### Reactive strength methods
- Drop jumps, hops/bounds, pogos, sprinting, fast-SSC plyometrics.
- Force-plate lens: RSI-Mod, RSI, contact time, jump height, reactive jump strategy.

### Sprint/speed tissue note (specificity tradeoff)
- Sprinting demands high force and tensile capacity. Too much HSR without enough sprint exposure may blunt max speed.
- Isometrics (e.g., run-specific isometric series) can maintain force production with less interference to sprint performance on certain days/phases.

### Technology inside the session (not only on test days)
- **Entry:** 3 CMJs after structured warm-up.
- **Within session:** ForceFrame/NordBord Training Mode for target force/intensity on isometrics/eccentrics.
- **Substitution:** max-effort isometric (e.g., seated ankle PF) in place of a set → data + stimulus.
- **Exit:** repeat assessment for acute neuromuscular response (useful for response insight; do not mix with baseline profiling).
- Early plyos (DJ/CMJ) can both monitor fatigue and provide a power stimulus.
- Max-effort isometrics (IMTP / isometric squat) can serve as PAPE *and* strength benchmark.

### Warm-up / data quality rules when testing in sessions
- Keep setup and warm-up consistent (RAMP common: raise, activate, mobilize, potentiate). Guide tip: test after mobilize, before potentiate, so potentiation does not muddy readiness reads.
- End-of-session testing reflects acute fatigue; do not treat it as fresh capacity.
- Team environments often inflate scores via competition/arousal; note context when comparing to quiet clinic/lab tests.

## Periodization / progression rules

### Shared value of periodization
- Periodized > non-periodized is supported; **no model is clearly superior to another**. Use structure for progressive overload and adaptation specificity; do not fetishize a brand of periodization.

### Hierarchy (BridgeAthletic-style language used in guide)
- **Program** = macrocycle / season (phases toward a long-term goal)
- **Phase** = week-to-week theme (e.g., strength or hypertrophy for **2–4 weeks**; phase periodization windows often **2–8 weeks**)
- **Workout** = session
- **Block** = consecutive exercises (superset, warm-up block, strength block, etc.)
- **Exercise** = single movement

### Continuum of variation
- **Non-varied:** same exercises/parameters/objectives; overload as athlete adapts. Sessions → weeks → program.
- Models (linear, undulating, phase) are points on a continuum of how much emphasis/intensity/volume varies over time—not totally different sciences.
- Session-to-session differences across models are small; organization of volume/intensity/emphasis over time is what differs.

### High–low model (in-season friendly)
- Stack high training stress on days that already have hard practice/games (e.g., heavy lifting same day as high sport load).
- Protect true low days for recovery/adaptation rather than spreading intensity evenly all week.

### Microdosing
- High frequency, lower load per exposure (e.g., **15–20 min** strength/speed/plyo several times/week vs 1–2 long **60–90 min** sessions).
- Use when time, recovery, or competition limit traditional sessions; maintains qualities with less session fatigue and less sport disruption.

### Linear periodization
- Higher volume / lower intensity → lower volume / higher intensity over time.
- Best fit called out: **novices** and **early-stage rehab** (simplicity).

### Undulating (DUP / WUP)
- Multiple emphases within a week or across successive weeks; useful when competing adaptations must be developed or several qualities maintained.
- **Hard programming cue from force plates:** if CMJ jump height is stable but RSI-Mod or braking RFD shifts meaningfully, consider a DUP/WUP cycle to rebalance weekly workload across qualities.

### Phase (block) periodization
- Short phases emphasizing few qualities; sequence informed by reversibility / decay rates.
- Example pattern in guide: traditional linear strength progression → power/speed block with reduced load and volume.
- Test before shifting emphasis (e.g., confirm force production improved before moving hard into power).

### Calendar vs flexible sequencing
- Fixed calendar dates are optional; sequential playlists preserve intended order when availability, competition, or rehab timelines move.

### Progression mechanics
- Change **one** training component (intensity, volume, frequency) *or* one constraint (ROM, technique, velocity) when chasing a clear adaptation. Changing many variables at once muddies feedback.
- Resistance progression typically: intensity first, then strategic volume as adaptation shows.
- VBT can progress via load, velocity, or both (same load, faster velocity = adaptation even if bar weight unchanged).
- When meaningful adaptation stalls under any model: temporary workload reduction / deload is appropriate.
- If monitoring causes **frequent** priority flips, the overall workload or structure—not the athlete—is likely wrong.

### Low-tech autoregulation (when plates/VBT unavailable)
- **2-for-2 rule:** if athlete does ≥2 extra reps above target on the final set in **two consecutive sessions**, increase load.
- Use RPE / RIR to adjust intensity from perceived effort.

## Constraints, dose, and load management

### Dose for strength (MED framing from 2003–2005 meta-analyses cited)
- Most **trained** athletes: intensities **>80% 1RM**, **2–4 sets** of **2–5 reps** per muscle group, **2–3×/week**, to establish/build muscular strength.
- Respect specificity, progressive overload, and interference; enough volume/intensity/frequency to adapt without exceeding capacity.
- Resistance training fatigue can suppress performance **12–72 hours** post-session; dose with that window in mind.

### VBT velocity-loss targets (immediate feedback / set termination)

| Velocity loss | Intent | Example targets in guide | Rationale |
|---------------|--------|--------------------------|-----------|
| **~10%** | Power | Target 1.0 m/s → cutoff 0.9 m/s | Max intent, high velocity, low fatigue |
| **~20%** | Strength | Target 0.5 m/s → cutoff 0.4 m/s | Heavier/slower; more loss often adds needless fatigue |
| **~30%** | Hypertrophy / muscular endurance | Target 0.6 m/s → cutoff 0.4 m/s | Higher volume; moderate loads, higher reps |

### Power velocity zones (bar velocity)
- **Strength–speed:** 0.75–1.0 m/s
- **Speed–strength:** 1.0–1.3 m/s
- No universal power threshold; set zones + loss cutoffs so intent matches the adaptation.

### Load vs load-response (program different decisions)
- **Load monitoring:** work done (volume, sprint distance, duration, competition exposure).
- **Load-response monitoring:** how the athlete adapted (fatigue, readiness, longer-term physical change).
- Example pairing: training log / athlete comparison = load; next-day CMJ on force plates = response.

### External vs internal load
- **External:** distance, weight, sprint volume, jump count.
- **Internal:** HR, session RPE, soreness, fatigue.
- Same external load ≠ same internal cost across athletes or across days for one athlete.
- Average concentric velocity at a given load sits at the intersection: reflects output (external) and, when tracked at a standardized load, response/tolerance.

### Training monotony (Foster)
- Repeated similar high loads with little variation can raise strain/fatigue and blunt adaptation even when total workload is unchanged. Vary foci; do not stack identical high days endlessly.

### Monitoring quality bar
- Minimally invasive (jump/isometric often better than disruptive biomarkers for day-to-day decisions).
- Reliable (stable when status unchanged).
- Sensitive (detects real readiness/fatigue/adaptation change).
- Match cadence to setting (elite: daily/weekly CMJ readiness; clinical: periodic hip adduction / single-leg balance, etc.).

### Reporting constraint
- More metrics ≠ better decisions. Keep a small KPI set aligned to qualities and decisions. Example dashboard KPIs cited: ForceDecks RSI-Mod (CMJ); ForceFrame hip add:abd ratio (45°); NordBord relative eccentric peak force (Nordic).

## Youth / athlete application notes

Guide content on youth is limited; lock only what appears:

- Same decision framework applies across elite, youth, and general clients (expert quotes + “Who Is This Guide For?”): assess → measure → check if the program is working.
- **Linear periodization** called out as suitable for **novice** trainees (and early rehab) because of simplicity.
- **Developing / in-season athletes:** prefer simpler power/strength menus; advanced contrast/superset volume is more for established/off-season athletes.
- **High school case (Cooper Napoli / undersized HS football):**
  - Do not bank a whole off-season on changing adolescent size; prioritize controllable KPIs.
  - Strategy: **speed + availability**.
  - KPIs: 20-yd and 40-yd times (SmartSpeed), early- vs late-acceleration splits.
  - Weight room supports sprinting: lower-body strength, power, force production; unilateral strength (e.g., Zercher Bulgarian split squat) plus hip flexor/adductor work for robustness.
  - Individualize around a shared team framework; track completion.
  - Outcome narrative in guide: ~0.3 s improvement per quarter on sprint times on average; athletes with **>80%** session attendance stayed available all season; deeper playoff run than prior five years (multi-factor; treat as case evidence, not a guaranteed recipe).
- **Team scale (LA Rams case):** shared framework → positional groups → individual mods from profiles (e.g., strength- vs power-dominant on ForceDecks). ~**60–70%** of roster got some individualization; continuity off-site via delivered programs mattered for camp readiness/body composition (staff attribution; multi-factor).

## Testing → prescription (when metrics change the lift plan)

Keep curve taxonomy light. Only hard or near-hard programming links from this PDF:

### Profiling that redirects emphasis
- **NordBord / ForceFrame / DynaMo / ForceDecks:** identify *which* strength problem exists (e.g., hip adductor weakness in a soccer player; persistent post-injury hamstring deficit; low CMJ force production despite OK jump height). Prioritize the deficit; do not add random accessories.
- **Quadrant-style pairing (referenced):** compare hip adduction strength vs eccentric hamstring strength to prioritize relative need.
- **Power limitation split:** CMJ high force / low height vs high height / low force; **DSI** to see whether maximal or ballistic force is the bigger limiter → choose max-strength vs ballistic/plyo emphasis.
- **Norms / benchmarks:** if a quality sits poorly vs sport-relevant norms, that quality warrants further attention in the next block.
- **Phase gate:** do not advance hard into power until force-production assessments show the intended strength adaptation.

### Monitoring that changes short-term prescription
- Stable CMJ height but changing **RSI-Mod** or **braking RFD** → consider DUP/WUP to rebalance qualities within the week.
- Velocity at a **standardized load** dropping → athlete may be less ready / more fatigued; adjust intensity or volume that day (VBT individuality).
- Hitting velocity-loss cutoff early → stop the set / end the high-quality stimulus; do not grind past intent.
- Alternative prescriptions when periodic assessment is clearly better or worse than baseline (guide points to programming platforms for alt prescriptions).
- Priorities flipping every monitor session → fix program structure/workload, do not endlessly chase the last test.

### Metric roles (ForceDecks / NordBord / ForceFrame table in guide)

| Tech | Common test | Monitoring examples | Profiling examples |
|------|-------------|---------------------|--------------------|
| ForceDecks | CMJ | RSI-Mod; contraction time; FT:CT | Jump height; concentric impulse 100 ms; eccentric deceleration impulse |
| NordBord | Nordic | Peak force asymmetry; peak force | Peak force / BM; peak force |
| ForceFrame | Hip add/abd 60° | Add/abd peak force; asymmetries | Add:abd ratio; add peak force; abd peak force |

### Feedback types to combine (not tech alone)
1. Assessment (objective)
2. Conversation (fatigue, soreness, confidence, pain, readiness)
3. Training/competition performance
4. Observation (strategy, technique, effort, coordination)

Immediate feedback optimizes the session; long-term trends decide progression/regression/direction. Separate day-to-day noise from real change.

### What testing does *not* do
- Does not replace practitioner judgment.
- Collecting data ≠ using data. Always ask: What does this say about the athlete, and what will you do differently?

## Hard stops / what NOT to do from the guide

- Do **not** design without a needs analysis and stated outcomes.
- Do **not** treat KPIs/outcomes as single-quality problems; mechanisms matter for repeatability.
- Do **not** change many variables at once when seeking a clear adaptation signal.
- Do **not** confuse sport specificity with copying sport skills in the weight room.
- Do **not** select exercises randomly or rotate novelty for its own sake.
- Do **not** chase fatigue instead of adaptation.
- Do **not** focus only on performance outputs while ignoring the qualities that drive them.
- Do **not** overrely on proxy measures without knowing their limits.
- Do **not** program from assumptions when feedback loops are absent.
- Do **not** stack high monotony (same high loads repeatedly) and expect clean adaptation.
- Do **not** flood dashboards with dozens of metrics; decide from a small KPI set.
- Do **not** use end-of-session fatigued tests as fresh capacity profiles.
- Do **not** assume one periodization model is universally best.
- Do **not** flood sprinters with HSR at the expense of sprint exposure if max speed is the priority (consider isometric adjuncts).
- Do **not** flip training priorities every time a monitor wavers; that flags a broken plan structure.
- Do **not** equate load completed with athlete response; monitor both.

## Quick wrap checklist (Strength Coach — Achieve programs)

Use when wrapping athlete programs (draft recommendations for Ryne / TrainHeroic):

1. **Needs locked?** Sport demands → bottlenecks → 2–4 KPIs with test methods and norms.
2. **Phase intent clear?** One primary quality (or small set) for 2–8 weeks; know what “done” looks like on tests.
3. **Session order set?** Explosives early → compounds → accessories (unless deliberate PAPE with recovery time).
4. **One progression lever?** Intensity *or* volume *or* frequency *or* one constraint this microcycle—not all four.
5. **Dose sane?** Trained strength: >80% 1RM, 2–4 × 2–5, 2–3×/wk as a MED starting point; respect 12–72 h fatigue.
6. **Power/reactive covered if needed?** Combine HSR + plyos when jump/power is a goal; match slow vs fast SSC to contact-time demands; use VBT zones/loss if available.
7. **In-season shape?** High–low stacking or microdosing if calendar is tight; keep simple menus for developing athletes.
8. **Load + response pair?** External work planned *and* a readiness/response check (CMJ metrics, velocity at fixed load, wellness)—with alt prescriptions if clearly down.
9. **Individualization path?** Shared framework → group → profile-based mods (strength- vs power-limited, asymmetry, tissue deficits)—not 30 unique philosophies.
10. **Hard stops reviewed?** No novelty chasing, no monotony stacks, no metric soup, no fatigued “baselines,” no HSR drowning sprint work when speed is the KPI.
11. **Youth/HS special?** Controllable KPIs (speed, availability, force) over size miracles; track attendance/completion as part of the program.
12. **Close the loop:** After the block, did the chosen KPIs move for the reasons you intended? If not, change stimulus or dose—not just the exercise name.
