                                           Foreword
I first began working on this project the summer of 2010. It had been three years since we
published our last CD and since then PRI had developed two new courses, Pelvis Restoration and
Interdisciplinary Integration. With the introduction of these courses and the addition of new
course speakers, the demand for an updated CD was mounting. When I began wrapping my
hands around this project, I never envisioned where it would end up today. It has taken two
years, two babies and two new PRI employees to fully complete our final publication of the Non-
Manual Techniques CD collection. The techniques within this lifetime collection began in 1980
with a 90-90 Supported Left Hemibridge as a way to unload the temporomandibular joint by
facilitating a left hamstring in patients who demonstrated compensatory forward head posture.
This and many other techniques were developed while Ron Hruska worked as a Director of a
local hospital physical therapy department. In 1999, he opened a private practice and established
the Postural Restoration Institute® where he continued to generate hundreds of additional
techniques, including the most recent, Long Seated Alternating Crossovers, (developed in July
2012) to facilitate a hip flexor as a lumbar spine rotator with the contralateral ischial seat
stationary on the floor as a reference center. During this 32 year time span, more than 500
techniques have been created based on the clinical experience of Ron Hruska and the physical
therapists of the Hruska Clinic™ while working with individual patients.

PRI offers three introductory courses; Myokinematic Restoration, Pelvis Restoration and
Postural Respiration. These introductory courses now have a corresponding Non-Manual
Techniques CD that is organized by muscle, inhibition and integration and placed in an order in
which you would want to isolate, inhibit or integrate. Also included on each CD is the
Integration program, which is comprised of more than 300 techniques in nine different positions.
In the Integration program, you will find a symbol next to each technique that is used to identify
whether that technique is a Myokinematic Restoration, Pelvis Restoration or a Postural
Respiration integrated activity. This symbol recognizes techniques that integrate the thorax with
the pelvis via the abdominals. These techniques are designed to inhibit a pattern and facilitate a
position by integrating abdominal activity. In other words, every time you see this symbol you
have chosen a technique that integrates the abdominals. In addition, while looking through
muscle and inhibition programs you will find an integration symbol next to techniques that are
considered an integrated activity to help the viewer cross-reference and identify integrated
activities. Also included are the top 10 repositioning techniques for each respective introductory
course. Another unique addition to this collection is the identification of reference centers.
Reference centers include the left abdominals, left ischial seat (sit bone), left heel and right arch.

These reference centers are identified on the Integration techniques found in the sidelying, short
seated, long seated, seated, standing and kneeling positions. Hundreds of hours have been spent
on arranging placement of techniques, organizing progression based on neuro-motor demands,
writing easy-to-understand instructions, identifying integrated activity, cross-referencing
techniques and adding reference centers to help the clinician select appropriate techniques for
treatment programs.
        - Bobbie Rappl, PTA, PRC
          (written in 2012 upon release of the PRI Non-Manual Techniques CD Collection)

Highlights of the Non-Manual Technique Programs:

      •   All programs found on the Myokinematic Restoration and Pelvis Restoration Programs follow
          the same order of progression based on neuro-motor demands as found in the Integration
          program, with the exception of Right Gluteus Maximus on the Pelvis Restoration Program and
          all of the Inhibition programs (for example the 90-90 Supported Hip Lift with Hemibridge will
          always precede 90-90 Hip Lift with Balloon).

      •   Right Gluteus Maximus is the only muscle that is found in two different Programs (Myokine-
          matic Restoration and Pelvis Restoration) and also follows a different order of progression and
          position on each.

      •   With regards to order of progression of the Inhibition programs, bilateral Inhibition will always
          precede unilateral Inhibition.

      •   If at first glance a technique appears to be placed in the wrong muscle program, consider that
          this specific muscle could be facilitated through inhibitory muscle, position or process used to
          isolate the desired muscle.

      •   These Non-Manual Techniques Programs uses terminology in the titles of the techniques that
          reflects consistent theoretical application among all PRI techniques.

      •   Respiratory refers to an action with a phase of respiration which expands either the left
          posterior mediastinum, left posterior outlet or left posterior hip capsule.

      •   Reciprocal reflects going through full range of available motion in both directions (for example
          full flexion followed by full extension is a reciprocal activity).

      •   Alternating reflects movement toward one side followed by movement toward the opposite side
          (for example performing the activity for three breaths on the left side followed by three breaths
          on the right side is an alternating activity).

      •   Supported means proprioceptive input gained from these surfaces in the following positions:
           o Standing: against a wall or placing your hands on a table.
           o Supine: with one foot in contact with the floor or wall.
           o Seated: against a wall, one foot in contact with the floor or hands on a table.
           o Sidelying: with your hip supported on a pillow or one foot in contact with the wall.

      •   Resisted refers to resistance coming from a band.


      •   Weighted refers to resistance coming from a weight.

PRI CD Organization Questions:

Q: Why is the PRI CD non-manual technique activity organized in the order of Right
Low Trap and Right Tricep, Left Low Trap and Left Serratus Anterior, Right Serratus
Anterior, and Right Subscapularis?

A: The typical Left Anterior Interior Chain (AIC) and Right Brachial Chain (BC) pattern involves a
pattern of pelvic, spinal and diaphragmatic orientation towards the right with the thorax rotating back
to the left above the diaphragm. This left upper trunk or rib cage rotation occurs to maintain frontal
plane balance and right upper extremity “reach” activities of the right dominant human.

Right handed activity also promotes left upper trunk rotation and places the right low trap, triceps,
and serratus anterior musculature in a position where normal contraction for scapula retraction, spinal
stabilization, and rib internal and external rotation is lost.

The right low trap and tricep repositions the scapula on the thorax through retraction and posterior
tilting of the scapula via a stabilized spine, left abdominal co-activation and extension of an elbow.
Once the scapula and rib cage are properly positioned, as a result of working the right low trap and
tricep, the left low trap and left serratus anterior musculature is needed to assist in the reorientation of
the upper thorax to the left and in stabilization of the upper quadrants during right arm reach.

After upper thoracic and spinal neutrality and stability is regained, the right serratus anterior is then
needed to stabilize the right scapular function during right arm reach, without requiring rotation of
the upper trunk to the left. Since most internal rotation of the right arm occurs from glenoid
movement and an overactive latissimus prior to this process, it would make sense to now retrain the
right subscapularis for humeral glenoid internal rotation independent of the latissimus after this neuro
re-education process.




Q: Why is the PRI CD activity organized in a specific, non-consistent order of position
for each desired muscle group?

A: The order of position progression varies with each muscular set of neuromotor retraining non-
manual PRI techniques. The order of position for each muscular set of non-manual techniques
reflects the demands placed on an individual to inhibit substitutional, alternative or accessory muscle
for desired acquired action. ‘Supine’ right low trap and right tricep acquired action, for example,
requires less inhibitory strategy than ‘sidelying’ or ‘prone’ positions.

In other words, as you proceed through the order of position progression, you will need to isolate
specific identified muscle while inhibiting agonistic effort at a greater level, since these later
positions promote more substitutional effort from undesirable muscle.

Q: Why is the PRI CD activity organized in the order of each individual non-manual
technique?

A: This order of non-manual techniques of each muscle and position section reflects PRI principles
of specific muscle isolation and integration through inhibition of antagonistic and appropriate
agonistic muscle that are overused. Therefore, non-manual techniques listed earlier in each section
maximally inhibit all muscle tone needed for specific muscle identification and isolated function.
This is done through feed-forward positioning, motor sequence direction, and inhalation and
exhalation phases of respiration. As you advance through each section the non-manual techniques
become more challenging neurologically due to increasing integration without using former
compensatory patterns to encode “new” discovered positional patterns of control. This re-training
and re-organization of sequential activity requires complete relaxation of muscles when neuro-motor
demands cease. As agonistic muscle is recruited in the later phases of each section, the need to
decrease agonistic and antagonistic tone at rest is a must before one can advance to another non-
manual technique. Therefore, processing through a position or a specific muscle program utilizing
PRI non-manual techniques requires the ability to isolate and integrate specific desired muscles
without fully inhibiting other tri-planar agonistic muscle, without recruiting antagonistic muscle and
without increasing hypertonic activity after muscle demands stop in the newly acquired ‘muscle
position’ to maintain position.

Each technique has been designed and described with specific muscle isolation and inhibition in
mind. Consequently, we advise each technique to be clinically used with accompanying therapist
instruction and guidance. Due to the specificity of each technique and in order to protect the science
of Postural Restoration, we ask that the PRI copyright remain only on material un-changed. Each
technique has been reviewed by numerous therapists for accuracy and maximized effectiveness.

For theory and explanation of treatment goals utilizing non-manual and manual PRI techniques,
please consider attendance of PRI sponsored courses or in-services. Upcoming dates and locations
can be found at www.posturalrestoration.com.

7. Standing – Best position for ‘re-biasing’ alternating lateralized cortical and centering sense

Supine
    •   Helps client sense ‘pressure’ or ‘weight’ distributed through feet, hips, shoulder/scapula
        and base of head.

    •   Real state of lordosis at neck and lumbar spine can be isolated, felt by clinician’s hands,
        and tension or degree of curves can be proprioceptively felt with appendage or axial
        movement.

    •   Patterned mechanics and autonomic tension can be sensed and comparison of bilateral
        function (TR, Hor Abd, Cerv Axial Rot, etc) can be objectively appreciated.

    •   Best position for autonomic nervous system treatment outcomes (Straight Leg Raise).

    •   Thoracic-scapula awareness and integrative sense is enhanced.

    •   Best position for cranial (sphenoid) and pelvis (sacrum) coronal axis rotation (tilting) in
        the sagittal plane.

    •   Offers ‘retraction’ sense at the sacrum (posterior outlets), sternum (posterior
        mediastinums), and sphenoid (posterior cerebellum).

    •   Most likely one of the first two positions recommended for facilitation of primary course
        opposition muscle because of bilateral comparative sense and facilitatory capability.

Supine 90-90
    •   Ideal position for hamstrings, glutes, abdominals, deep cervical flexors, cranial flexors
        (pterygoids and tongue) facilitation with concomitant inhibition of psoas, SCM and
        anterior temporalis.

    •   Ideal position to experience controllable diaphragmatic breathing and simultaneous effect
        on chest wall, sinus respiratory flow.

    •   Calcaneal, molar and occipital protuberance/nuchal ridge sense can be realized without
        effort.

    •   Best position to establish respiratory function, drainage, PRI balloon mastery, and
        internal rotation.

    •   Best position to realize acetabular function and bilateral AF and FA rotational sequences,
        integration and functional ability.

    •   ‘Reach’ of knees and elbows/hands can be experienced without over working lower
        quadrant transverse stabilizers.

    •   Isolates thoracic function and ribcage orientation.

    •   Best position for inhibitory (algorithmic) compensatory motor planning and patterning,
        because of “plantar perception”.

    •   One of the ‘middle’ positions recommended when using Myokinematic Restoration and

                                     Reference Centers
                                      - Key Concepts -

      •   Reference is a relation between objects in which one object (anatomical site)
          designates, or acts as a means by which to connect to or link to, another object
          (anatomical site).

      •   The first anatomical sensed site in this relation is said to refer to the second sensed site.

      •   So the relationship (referenced activity) between the right great toe and the left heel is
          right AF ER, via the right glute max.

      •   The form of reference is a sensorial perception or a tactile sensory perception.

      •   The second sensed site (left heel), the one to which the first sensed site (right great toe)
          refers, is called the referent of the first sensed site.

      •   For example, in the sentence, “The right great toe enables right AF ER with the help of
          the left heel”, the right great toe and the left heel are co-referential, since they both
          refer to the same outcome; right AF ER.


      •   To reproduce reference outcomes, sensorial tactile sites are needed to be experienced
          many times together and with limitation of other initiated simultaneous tactile sense.

      •   For example, introducing a left ab contraction with a right arm reach at the time of left
          ‘heel contact’ and right ‘toe off’ may weaken the reference and thus the relation.
       Is the technique you’re choosing capable of maintaining the strength of tactility?
      • PRI has formatted reference sites (usually two centers) that include the left abdominals,
         left ischial seat, left heel and right foot arch.

      •   These reference centers are identified on the Integration techniques found in the
          sidelying, short seated, long seated, seated, standing and kneeling positions.

      •   However, we should all select a primary site of reference that has a referent or two, to
          solidify how to experience, sense, repeat and describe the outcome or reference (for
          example: right intercostal inhibition).

5. Straighten your arms, bringing your hands to your side.

1. Stand against a desk or counter with both feet on a 2-inch block and a ball between
